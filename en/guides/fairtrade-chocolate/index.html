Choosing fair-trade chocolate

Fair trade has been discussed more intensely around cocoa than almost any other food category. Here is what the certifications actually cover.

Why chocolate in particular

Most of the world's cocoa comes from West Africa, principally Ivory Coast and Ghana. International bodies and NGOs have repeatedly reported on child labour and thin returns to farmers in this supply chain. Chocolate gains value at every processing step, and the structure of that chain tends to leave a small share with the cocoa grower. That is the background to why fair trade is discussed so specifically in this category.

The certifications and schemes

  • Fairtrade International (FLO): sets a minimum price for cocoa plus a Fairtrade Premium paid to the producer cooperative. The best known to consumers
  • Rainforest Alliance: merged with UTZ certification in 2018. Centred on environmental and farming-practice standards; does not include a Fairtrade-style minimum price
  • Direct trade / bean-to-bar: no single certifying body — what matters is how specifically the maker discloses the producer, region and price paid

The three rest on different premises and are not directly comparable. What matters is which one covers what you actually care about.

Cacao percentage is a separate claim

A “70% cacao” label and a fair-trade claim are unrelated axes. A higher percentage does not mean more of the price reaches the farmer. If both matter to you, check each separately.

Finding it in Japan

Bars and cocoa carrying the Fairtrade mark are sold by some mainstream manufacturers alongside natural-food shops and online retailers. Small bean-to-bar makers who roast from raw beans have also grown in number; many are uncertified but publish the farm and terms of trade in specific detail instead.

What to check

  • Which scheme, if any (FLO, Rainforest Alliance, none)
  • Without certification, whether the producer, region and terms are disclosed
  • Treat the cacao percentage as separate information from any certification
  • The source of secondary ingredients such as palm oil or emulsifiers — fair-trade terms usually cover the cocoa only
  • The packaging material — foil, paper, or plastic
